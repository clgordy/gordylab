Research Program

A program of biology education research built over a decade at NC State, integrating scholarship of teaching and learning (SOTL) with discipline-based education research (DBER) — anchored throughout by the study of belonging.

Belonging: The Anchor

Belonging — the subjective sense of connection and legitimacy as a valued member of a social setting (Strayhorn, 2019) — is a fundamental human need that comes only after physiological needs and safety. Students cannot meet their academic potential if their need for belonging is not met, which is why belonging anchors every project in this research program, whether studying a single pedagogical strategy or how faculty make decisions about their teaching.

Qualitative Case Study

Working with an interdisciplinary group of faculty, staff, graduate students, and undergraduates in the BioSci Belonging Research Group (founded 2022), the team carried out semi-structured interviews with 27 undergraduate life sciences majors, focused on their experiences within their classes and department, feelings of belonging, and perceptions of departmental DEI efforts.

Using Hurtado's Multicontextual Model of Diverse Learning Environments (MMDLE) as a theoretical framework, three themes emerged: students feel they belong when instructors and staff implement inclusive pedagogies, provide co-curricular resources and support, and work to humanize life sciences education — all approached with authenticity and intentionality.

Published as Harry et al. 2024, Humanizing STEM Education, in CBE-LSE, the field's top biology education research journal.

Belonging in Disabled & Neurodivergent Students

An emerging line of work focused specifically on belonging among disabled and neurodivergent students in STEM, including development of a new theoretical framework combining MMDLE with complex embodiment theory of disability, and a planned study of the impact of neurotype matching on mentor–mentee relationships — a largely unstudied dimension of research mentorship.

Pedagogical Innovation

Novel Guided Inquiry Learning (GIL)-based constructivist pedagogies that pair a structured, model-based exploration→concept-invention→application cycle with another active-learning strategy.

Tactile Teaching Tools + GIL (TTT-GIL)

Developed with Melissa Ramirez to represent dynamic 3D molecular interactions accessibly for all students, including those who are blind or visually impaired. Students explore 3D-printed molecular puzzles instead of 2D paper models, guided through exploration, concept invention, and application.

Evaluated at NC State and UNC-Pembroke (lac operon activity, published JMBE 2020), then scaled nationally through the NSF RCN-UBE STEM BUILD network (2020–2023, $74,983): 3 regional teams across NC, GA, and VA developed 4 TTT-GIL activities implemented at 8 institutions, impacting over 1,000 students and measurably increasing faculty confidence in Universal Design for Learning (UDL).

Case Studies + GIL

Pairs GIL-based technical content with case studies that engage learners in the societal context of the science — developed and assessed in GGA 250 (Genes, Race, and Society) and GGS 770 (the GG Scholars survey course for first-year PhD students across 15 programs and 4 colleges).

Ongoing data collection examines both topic-specific learning outcomes and changes in genetic determinist thinking, with multiple manuscripts expected within the year, alongside a broader study of how instructors elsewhere teach technical concepts in societal context.

GIL + Digital Manipulatives

Adapts K-12 digital manipulative technology (Seesaw) for asynchronous, constructivist learning of molecular biology in online undergraduate STEM courses, including a concept-mapping-based assessment strategy for teaching the central dogma (manuscripts in preparation).

Future Direction: How Do Instructors Choose?

A planned mixed-methods study of STEM instructors with experience across multiple active-learning strategies, using Situated Expectancy-Value Theory within the Comprehensive Faculty Development Model to understand how instructors choose among pedagogical strategies, when they create vs. adopt existing activities, and how faithfully they implement published designs.

Programmatic Structures

High-impact educational practices (HIEs) reliably improve academic and personal outcomes — and those impacts are amplified for students from marginalized communities, and additive across multiple experiences. Yet almost no published research directly compares the impact of one HIE structure against another. This research program addresses that gap.

Stand-Alone vs. Linked CUREs

A linked CURE connects multiple course sections around a shared research question, with ongoing cross-course communication and troubleshooting, compared to a stand-alone CURE contained within a single course.

The current Wax Worm Genetics Linked CURE (GN 312, 2024–present) studies how wax worms biodegrade plastic at the genetic level, linking in-person lab sections (saliva enzyme gene variation) with an online section (collaborative genome annotation), with student communication studied via the Yellowdig platform. A planned multi-semester redesign, co-developed with Dr. Nick Buchler and PhD student Toya Tanner, will gradually introduce cross-course linkage to directly compare stand-alone and linked models using a Communities of Practice framework.

Interdisciplinary Tiered Mentoring (ITM)

Piloted with Melissa Ramirez and Martha Burford Reiskind (GGA/GOHA seed funding), ITM organizes graduate mentors, three first-year undergraduate mentees, and a faculty advisor into interdisciplinary "mentoring families," with intensive equity-focused mentor training, weekly check-ins, and a focus on the societal impact of research.

The Wolfpack Solutions pilot (2023–24) recruited 3 graduate student mentors from 40 applicants; all have since moved into faculty or postdoctoral positions. A submitted NSF IUSE proposal ($747,694) proposes a randomized comparison of ITM against traditional undergraduate research mentoring across outcomes including interdisciplinarity, transition to college, intentionality, and community, using the Multicontextual Model for Diverse Learning Environments as a framework.

Additional Long-Term Work

Campus Food & Housing Insecurity Study

An ongoing mixed-methods study, now in its fourth iteration since 2018. Lead PI beginning in 2026, examining how basic needs insecurity affects students' participation in high-impact experiences like undergraduate research.

WPWS Data & a DBER CURE

Developing graduate research projects and a planned DBER CURE that draws on data collected since 2020 through Wicked Problems Wolfpack Solutions (WPWS), in which undergraduates learn qualitative and quantitative research skills while addressing real research questions.
